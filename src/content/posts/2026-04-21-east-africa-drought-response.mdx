The drought that struck the Horn of Africa between 2020 and 2023—the worst in forty years by some meteorological measures—killed an estimated 43,000 people, displaced over a million, and wiped out livestock herds that had taken generations to rebuild. It also happened in full view of a sophisticated early warning system that had been signalling danger for over a year before humanitarian response scaled.

That gap between warning and action has become the central focus of humanitarian reform in the region.

## What the old system did and didn't do

The FEWS NET (Famine Early Warning Systems Network) and IGAD Climate Prediction and Applications Centre had been issuing Integrated Food Security Phase Classification alerts at Crisis and Emergency levels for months before the 2022 famine declaration. Donors received the reports. Governments received them. Humanitarian agencies received them.

The funding mobilised too slowly and too late. The reasons were systemic:

- **Donor release mechanisms** required a formal emergency declaration that lagged the actual crisis by months
- **Government reluctance** to declare emergencies—for political and reputational reasons—delayed national response activation
- **Pre-positioned stocks** of food and non-food items were inadequate in remote pastoral areas
- **Targeting data** was outdated and based on administrative units too large to identify the most affected communities

## The reforms

Following the 2022 crisis, a coalition of donors—led by the United Kingdom, United States, and European Union—agreed to fund a new **Anticipatory Action framework** for the Horn of Africa. The framework pre-commits funding to specific response actions when drought indicators reach defined thresholds, before a formal emergency declaration.

In practice, this means:

- **Cash transfers** triggered automatically when rainfall deficits reach 60% of the seasonal average, giving pastoralist families resources before they sell distress livestock
- **Livestock destocking** support deployed when vegetation indices drop below critical levels, allowing herders to sell at market prices rather than distress prices
- **School feeding** programmes activated in advance of the lean season, maintaining nutritional status before deterioration becomes severe

The system went live across 14 districts in Ethiopia, Kenya, and Somalia in 2024. The 2025 assessment, published last month, found that early-action interventions reached 2.3 million people ahead of the short rains failure, and that acute malnutrition rates in covered districts were measurably lower than in comparable uncovered districts during the same period.

## What still doesn't work

The anticipatory action framework works for predictable seasonal shocks—the short rains failure, the long rains underperformance. It is less suited to compound crises where drought interacts with conflict, as in southern Somalia and parts of Ethiopia's Somali Region.

In conflict-affected areas, humanitarian access remains the binding constraint. Early warning without the ability to reach affected populations is surveillance without response. The political negotiations required to maintain humanitarian corridors in active conflict zones are separate from, and harder than, the technical work of improving drought forecasting.

**Community-level data** remains inadequate. The district-level classifications used for trigger definitions smooth over enormous variation within administrative units. A district classified at Crisis level may contain pockets of Catastrophe alongside areas at Stressed—and the people most in need are often the least visible in survey data.

## The longer horizon

Climate projections for the Horn of Africa consistently show increased frequency and intensity of drought events through mid-century. The anticipatory action framework is a necessary adaptation to that trajectory, but it is not sufficient alone.

Livestock system transformation—shifting from extensive pastoral systems toward more diversified livelihood strategies, without destroying the cultural and ecological knowledge embedded in pastoralism—is a decades-long process. Investment in water infrastructure, rangeland management, and drought-tolerant crop varieties must accompany the humanitarian system reforms if the region is to reduce rather than just manage drought vulnerability.
