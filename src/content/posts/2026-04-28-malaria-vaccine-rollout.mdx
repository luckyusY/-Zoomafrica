Health ministries across Ghana, Nigeria, and Côte d'Ivoire announced this week that cumulative doses of the R21/Matrix-M malaria vaccine have crossed the one million mark since the expanded rollout began in early 2024. Preliminary surveillance data from sentinel sites in all three countries suggest a 30–40% reduction in severe malaria cases among vaccinated children under five.

## Why this rollout is different

Previous malaria vaccine trials—most notably the RTS,S programme in Malawi, Ghana, and Kenya—demonstrated efficacy but faced significant delivery challenges: cold chain requirements, multi-dose schedules, and integration into already-stretched immunisation programmes.

R21 has an improved efficacy profile and has benefited from lessons learned in the earlier rollout. Key differences include:

- **Higher efficacy in seasonal transmission zones**, where the vaccine is given as a three-dose primary course ahead of the high-transmission season
- **Simplified cold chain** requirements compared to earlier formulations
- **Community health worker integration**, with trained frontline workers able to administer doses during routine child health visits rather than requiring specialist clinic attendance

## The political economy of vaccine rollout

Vaccine acceptance has been higher than early projections feared, in part because community engagement was built into programme design from the start. In northern Ghana and the Sahel belt of Nigeria, traditional leaders were briefed and involved before any doses were administered. Religious leaders in Muslim-majority communities—where historical mistrust of vaccination campaigns has sometimes slowed uptake—were engaged through faith-based health networks rather than government health ministry channels alone.

Funding for the rollout has come from a combination of domestic government budgets, Gavi the Vaccine Alliance disbursements, and bilateral contributions from France and the United Kingdom. The Serum Institute of India is manufacturing the vaccine at a target price of under four dollars per dose—a cost point that makes national budget-line inclusion feasible for lower-middle-income countries.

## What comes next

Scale remains the challenge. West Africa alone has over 30 million children under five in high-transmission zones. Reaching one million vaccinated children is a significant milestone, but it represents a fraction of the target population.

Health system capacity—trained vaccinators, functional cold chain logistics, birth registration that allows for age verification—varies widely within and between countries. The countries that have done best in the early rollout are those that invested in health system strengthening, not just vaccine procurement.

WHO and the Africa CDC are coordinating a 2026 review that will assess whether the rollout is on track to achieve the 80% coverage target in enrolled districts by 2028. Early indicators suggest that target is achievable in urban and peri-urban areas but will require significant additional investment in remote rural health posts.
