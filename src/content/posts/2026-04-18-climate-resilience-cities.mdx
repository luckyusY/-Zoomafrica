Extreme heat, flooding, and water stress are reshaping city planning across Africa. The strongest strategies treat resilience as a **systems problem**: infrastructure, public health, and land use moving together rather than as separate departmental programmes.

## The scale of the challenge

By 2050, more than half of Africans will live in cities. Many of those cities are already experiencing 30–40 additional hot days per year compared to the 1990 baseline. Flooding events that once struck once per generation now arrive every few years.

The compounding effect is significant. Heat increases water demand exactly when supply is most stressed. Flooding damages the roads needed for emergency response. Poor air quality from vehicle congestion worsens respiratory illness exactly when extreme heat is also straining hospitals.

## Key adaptation moves

### Heat mapping and targeted shade corridors

Several cities have begun using thermal satellite imagery to identify urban heat islands—often informal settlements with dense, low-albedo rooftops and no tree cover. Targeted interventions include:

- **Cool roof subsidies** for low-income households
- **Street-tree planting programmes** prioritized by heat risk
- **Revised building codes** requiring minimum shading for new commercial construction

### Stormwater capture in public space design

Nairobi, Lagos, and Dar es Salaam are piloting **sponge city** design elements: permeable paving in parks, bioswales alongside major roads, and retention basins that double as recreational areas in dry season. The dual-use framing helps unlock municipal budget that would otherwise not reach flood management.

### Early-warning and community response networks

Meteorological agencies in Kenya, Ghana, and Senegal have expanded hyperlocal weather forecasting to neighbourhood scale. When combined with community liaison networks—often organized through women's groups and church associations—last-mile warning systems can move faster than official emergency services.

## What cities need most

Finance remains the central constraint. Climate adaptation infrastructure does not generate revenue in the way that a toll road or a power station does. Closing the funding gap will require:

- Sovereign green bond markets that ring-fence adaptation spending
- Regional pooling mechanisms for disaster response
- Concessional lending from multilateral development banks tied to credible adaptation plans rather than project-by-project approvals

The cities that are moving fastest are those that have integrated climate risk into every major capital budget decision—not those waiting for a standalone "resilience project" to arrive.
