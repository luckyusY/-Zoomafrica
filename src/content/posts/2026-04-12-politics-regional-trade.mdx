Trade policy is often discussed like a switch—on or off. In practice, it is a long sequence of operational and political decisions: standards harmonization, data sharing, dispute resolution, and infrastructure investment that must all move in the same direction at roughly the same time.

## The AfCFTA in year three

The African Continental Free Trade Area has now been operational long enough for early patterns to emerge. Intra-African trade as a share of total trade has ticked upward, but the change remains modest. The structural barriers—poor road and rail connectivity, fragmented customs systems, non-tariff barriers that take years to identify and negotiate away—do not yield to a single political agreement.

What the AfCFTA has done more effectively is create a **forum and a deadline** that keeps trade ministers and technical officials meeting regularly. That convening function matters more than it appears in the aggregate trade statistics.

## Where progress is fastest

The corridors that are genuinely improving are those where:

- **A single country is driving implementation** with political will at the highest level
- **Private sector logistics companies** have made capital commitments that give them a stake in smoother border crossing
- **Digital customs systems** have been harmonized to allow pre-clearance, reducing truck dwell times from days to hours

The Northern Corridor linking Kenya, Uganda, Rwanda, and the eastern DRC has seen measurable improvement on all three dimensions. Dwell times at Mombasa port have fallen. Trucking companies report fewer unofficial payments. Cross-border trade in fresh produce—time-sensitive and therefore a good stress test—has grown.

## The politics underneath

None of this happens automatically. The officials who negotiate border procedures, manage customs IT systems, and coordinate with private sector logistics associations work largely out of public view. Their counterparts across borders often have different mandates, different technology stacks, and different political incentives.

Building the trust that lets two border agencies share live data—or agree on a mutual recognition arrangement for health certificates—takes years of relationship-building at technical and political levels simultaneously.

Regional economic communities like the East African Community, ECOWAS, and SADC provide the institutional scaffolding for this work. Their effectiveness depends less on their formal mandates than on the quality of their secretariats and the political backing they receive from member state heads of government.

## The next decade

The most important trade infrastructure investment most African countries could make is not a port or a road—it is a **capable, well-resourced, and politically independent trade facilitation agency** with clear mandate to identify and systematically eliminate non-tariff barriers.

That is unglamorous, technocratic work. It does not cut ribbons. But it compounds.
