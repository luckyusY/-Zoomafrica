Cape Verde sits in the Atlantic, 570 kilometres off the coast of Senegal, an archipelago of ten volcanic islands that has built one of Africa's more successful small-island economies. Tourism and remittances from a large diaspora in Portugal, the Netherlands, and the United States underpin a GDP per capita that is among the highest in West Africa.

Now the government in Praia is advancing a Blue Economy Strategy—a framework for managing ocean-based economic activities in a way that is both productive and sustainable. The conversation is as much about social equity as it is about fish stocks.

## Tourism's double-edged growth

Visitor arrivals to Cape Verde grew to 1.2 million in 2025, up from 800,000 in 2019 and recovering strongly from the pandemic collapse. Sal and Boa Vista islands, with their white-sand beaches and direct charter flights from European capitals, absorb the majority of visitors in resort complexes that are largely all-inclusive.

The economic leakage from all-inclusive resort tourism is significant: food is often imported, staff are sometimes brought in for management roles, and profits flow to European or South African hotel groups. Local entrepreneurs and fishing communities on less-visited islands see limited benefit from the visitor numbers counted at Sal airport.

The tourism ministry is pushing a deliberate **island diversification strategy** that channels marketing spend toward Santo Antão—known for dramatic hiking terrain—and São Vicente, home to Mindelo's celebrated music and arts culture. The goal is to build a tourism product where visitors stay longer, spend more locally, and distribute that spending across multiple islands.

## Fishing under pressure

Artisanal fishing is the oldest industry in the islands and still a central part of cultural identity on communities across the archipelago. Small-scale fishers using traditional wooden boats—lanchas—target tuna, grouper, and lobster with methods that have changed little in generations.

The pressure on those fisheries has intensified. Industrial fishing vessels, some licensed by the Cape Verdean government and some operating illegally in the exclusive economic zone, have reduced stock abundance in nearshore waters that artisanal fishers depend on. Sea surface temperature increases linked to climate change have altered the seasonal migration patterns of tuna, requiring longer trips and more fuel.

The Blue Economy Strategy sets a target of **30% marine protected area coverage** by 2030—up from approximately 8% today. Artisanal fishing communities have been involved in the design of proposed MPAs, with no-take zones matched by community management zones where traditional fishing methods are permitted.

## Whale watching as a conservation asset

Humpback whales use Cape Verdean waters as a breeding and nursing ground between December and April. A small but growing whale-watching industry has emerged around Boa Vista and São Nicolau, providing income for former fishers who have transitioned to boat-based tourism guides.

Biologists from the Cape Verde Whale Research Project have documented one of the largest humpback populations in the Atlantic—a population that had not been studied systematically before 2010. The economic value of a living whale to the tourism economy is now a concrete number that conservation advocates can put in front of finance ministry officials.

## The governance challenge

Managing a blue economy across ten islands, with artisanal fishing communities on each, industrial licence holders, tourism investors, and an internationally important marine ecosystem all competing for the same ocean is genuinely complex governance work.

Cape Verde's smallness is both a constraint and an advantage. The country cannot drive policy at the scale of West Africa's major economies. But it can move quickly, experiment with policy design, and demonstrate models that larger countries might later adapt. On marine protected area design, community fishing agreements, and blue bond financing, Cape Verde is punching well above its weight in international ocean governance discussions.
