The African Union's Great Green Wall initiative—an ambitious plan to restore 100 million hectares of degraded land across the Sahel by 2030—passed its 2025 progress review last month with a mixed but broadly encouraging picture. Four of the eight priority countries are reporting above-target land restoration, while Mauritania and Chad face significant shortfalls linked to insecurity and implementation funding gaps.

## What the initiative has achieved

The Great Green Wall is frequently mischaracterised as a single wall of trees. In practice, it is a mosaic of interventions: farmer-managed natural regeneration, community nurseries, soil and water conservation structures, and agroforestry systems that integrate trees into farmland rather than creating separate forest plantations.

Senegal, Ethiopia, and Niger have become global models for what community-led restoration can achieve at scale. In the Zinder region of Niger, satellite analysis shows millions of trees that were invisible in 1980s imagery—regrown through a technique called Farmer-Managed Natural Regeneration (FMNR) that costs almost nothing and requires no external inputs.

Key achievements across the initiative as of April 2026:

- **18 million hectares** of land restored or under improved management
- **350,000 jobs** created in restoration and associated supply chains
- **5 million people** with improved food security in restored landscapes according to programme monitoring data
- **12 million hectares** of soil with improved carbon sequestration capacity

## Why Niger and Senegal matter most to the story

The most striking demonstration sites are in the Sahel countries where land degradation was most severe. In Senegal's Casamance region, restored mangrove systems have rebuilt fish nursery habitat that directly supports artisanal fishing communities. In Niger, restored farmland now produces 500,000 additional tonnes of food grain per year compared to pre-restoration baselines.

These are not abstract ecosystem services. They are the difference between food security and seasonal hunger for millions of households in some of the world's poorest landscapes.

## The gaps

Progress has been uneven, and the initiative faces four persistent challenges:

**Tenure insecurity** undermines restoration investment. Farmers who do not have secure rights to the trees they grow have less incentive to protect them. Land tenure reform—slow, politically contested, deeply local—is the prerequisite that no amount of technical assistance can substitute for.

**Conflict zones** in the Sahel belt have made restoration impossible in significant areas of Burkina Faso, Mali, and parts of Niger. The security situation has deteriorated since the initiative was launched.

**Monitoring and verification** remain inconsistent across countries. Some national programmes use satellite monitoring; others rely on self-reported field data. Independent verification capacity needs to expand.

**Long-term maintenance funding** is not secure. Trees planted need tending for years before they are self-sustaining. Donor funding cycles do not always align with ecological timelines.

## The 2030 outlook

At the current trajectory, the Great Green Wall is on track to restore between 40 and 60 million hectares by 2030—well short of the 100 million hectare target, but a transformative achievement if the quality of restoration holds. The distinction between restored land that supports communities and planted trees that die in the first dry season is everything.
