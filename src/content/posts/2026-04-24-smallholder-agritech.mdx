Smallholder farmers—those working plots of two hectares or less—produce roughly 70% of the food consumed in sub-Saharan Africa. They do so largely without access to the information, finance, and inputs that farmers in wealthier countries take for granted. A new generation of agri-tech platforms is attempting to close that gap at the scale that actually matters.

## The problem with the previous generation of agri-tech

The 2010s saw a wave of venture-backed agri-tech startups targeting African smallholders. Most of them failed, or achieved modest scale and then stagnated. The post-mortems revealed common patterns:

- Products designed around smartphone interfaces in populations where feature phones dominate
- Business models that required farmers to pay upfront in cash-constrained environments
- Extension services delivered in English or official languages when farmers work in local languages
- No integration with existing informal market systems that farmers already trusted

The startups that survived—and a few that have reached genuine scale—share a different set of design choices.

## What scale looks like now

**Apollo Agriculture** in Kenya reaches over 300,000 smallholder maize farmers with bundled services: satellite soil analysis, customised input recommendations, input financing, and crop insurance structured as a single product that a farmer signs up for with a phone call. The satellite soil analysis component—which previously would have required expensive field testing—is delivered via machine learning models trained on decades of soil sample data.

**OAF (One Acre Fund)** has long been the benchmark for smallholder service delivery, operating in eight countries with over 4 million farmer clients. Its model—field-based extension officers, in-kind input loans repaid from harvest—is expensive per farmer but achieves measurable yield improvements.

**Wefarm** built a peer-to-peer farmer knowledge network over SMS, allowing smallholders to ask agricultural questions and receive answers from other farmers. It now has over 4 million users and has demonstrated that farmer-to-farmer knowledge transfer works at scale and is trusted in ways that government extension services often are not.

## Satellite and remote sensing

Remote sensing has transformed what is possible at low cost. Platforms like Gro Intelligence and Farmerline use satellite imagery, weather data, and AI to provide:

- **Crop health monitoring** identifying stress patches before they cause yield loss
- **Planting window recommendations** tailored to local microclimate
- **Harvest yield forecasting** that helps farmers and aggregators plan logistics

The data is increasingly available in near-real-time and at 3–10 metre resolution, meaning individual farm-level analysis is now feasible across entire regions.

## The financing piece

Input financing—lending farmers the cost of seeds and fertiliser against the next harvest—has been the missing piece that limits uptake of improved practices. Without credit, even a farmer who knows that hybrid seed would improve their yield cannot afford it at planting time.

Mobile-based credit products, often structured as insurance-backed loans that are forgiven in drought years, are reaching farmers through partnerships with mobile network operators and mobile money platforms. The cost of underwriting has fallen as better data on farmer creditworthiness becomes available through transaction history and satellite-verified crop progress.

## What still needs to happen

**Last-mile distribution** of physical inputs—seeds, fertiliser, crop protection—remains broken in many areas. Digital platforms can get information and finance to farmers; getting the right product to the right village at the right time before planting is a logistics problem that technology alone cannot solve.

**Market linkages** matter as much as production inputs. A farmer who increases yield but cannot find a buyer at a fair price is worse off—they have incurred costs for no benefit. Agri-tech platforms that close the loop from production through aggregation to buyer are the ones most likely to build durable businesses.

**Women farmers**, who comprise 60–80% of food producers in many African countries, are systematically underserved by agri-tech products designed around male household heads. Platforms that reach women specifically—through women's groups, female extension officers, and products that address women's land tenure constraints—consistently report better outcomes.
